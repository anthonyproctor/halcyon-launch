Monday, January 10, 2061. 5:40 AM. The tablet on the kitchen table made the two note chime it only made for Mars, and Sam Okafor, in his socks at the counter, said "Okay," to it out loud, which was a thing he'd started doing and didn't love. The coffee maker had been slow since Thanksgiving. He kept meaning to replace it. He kept not doing it, because Nneka had bought it, and he'd never read the manual, and as long as it worked he wouldn't have to.
He had an order for this by now. Coffee. The chair by the back window, where the yard was still dark and the neighbor's motion light clicked on for a rabbit, then for the same rabbit again. Then the tablet. The log had left Jezero crater ten minutes and seven seconds before it got to Aurora. Before that it had sat in the relay queue most of the night, so Ada had said every word of it hours ago, at the end of her shift, on a planet where it was already dark again. Nothing she said was news by the time he heard it. He'd stopped minding that. Mostly.
Ada Okafor. Ares Station. Sol 3518. One way, ten minutes, seven seconds.
"Hi, Dad. Sol 3518, end of shift. Mom's watch says 5:15 in the morning your time, so you're up, and the coffee maker's doing that thing where it sighs at you. Get the coffee. I'll wait. I mean, I won't, I'm recorded, but you know what I mean."
"Good news first. Basil. I've got eleven plants going in cut down sample tubes on the shelf over my bunk, and Dr. Lindgren in medical says it's a fire code violation. I asked her which fire department she was going to call. She took one home."
"Okay. Real stuff. Stack four lost another two percent this week. That's the third week in a row. Hollis's report to Earth says the plant is operating 'within projected degradation,' which is true if you projected it on a napkin. I took the actual cell voltages off the plant log and ran the curve myself, three times, on three different fits, because I know what you're going to ask. I get 952 days of margin. His report says 1,012. Sixty days is a lot of days, Dad. It's two months of everybody breathing."
"Don't tell Hollis I said that. Actually, tell him. No. Don't. I'll tell him. I'll tell him tomorrow."
"Your last log. You did the voice. The 'everything's fine, kiddo, the people over here are top notch' voice. You used that voice for all of junior high. I know what it means. Don't do the voice, Dad. I'm an engineer on a planet that's trying to kill me. I can take a number."
"And I know Bea's job is a mess, because the feed says 'Halcyon program reaffirms schedule' every four days, and nobody reaffirms anything that's fine."
"Love you. My number's 952. Ada out."
He watched it twice. The second time he paused it where she held up four fingers for stack four, because her nails were bitten down the way they'd been in eighth grade, the year her mother got sick, and he hadn't seen them like that since.
Then he got the gas bill out of the drawer by the phone charger, the drawer that still had Nneka's reading glasses in it, folded, on top of the takeout menus, and turned the bill over and wrote 952 on the back. Under it he wrote 969. Everyone at Tallgrass knew that one without thinking: the number of days from this morning to September 6, 2063, when the Halcyon was supposed to reach Mars, if it left on February 18 of the year after next, if it left at all. He subtracted. Then he did it again, the way you check a torque value you don't like.
Minus seventeen.
The ARA's number, the one in Hollis Bayne's reports and on the agency's website and in every briefing Sam had sat in the back of, left the colony forty three days to spare. Ada's number ran the air out seventeen days before the ship got there. He sat with the bill in his hand until the coffee went cold. Then he put it under the fruit bowl, face down, and went to work.
Tuesday, January 11. Sam got in at six, before the cleaning crew had finished the second floor, and sat down at Bea's desk because it was the only desk in the building with the whole program on it. Her monitor had a sticky note on the bezel in her square capitals: ASK W ABOUT STAND. There was a mug of pens, a box of tissues with one pulled halfway out, and a printout of the integrated master schedule, all thirty one thousand lines of it, folded open to the departure stage with a coffee ring over February 2063.
By 6:40 he'd found the status deck she'd presented on Friday. Every line on it was green. He'd also found the risk register, four hundred and twelve open risks, the newest of them added in October, and an issues log that stopped in the middle of November like somebody had put the pen down halfway through a sentence.
At 7:15 Quinn came in with two coffees and set one in front of him without asking how he took it. She'd guessed right, which he noticed. "Garrett wants a plan by Friday. Hale's office wants a status by Wednesday. Rafa and Hana want to know if they still have to come to Bea's Tuesday meeting, which nobody liked, so I told them probably not."
She waited. Sam realized she was waiting for him to tell her what they were doing first.
